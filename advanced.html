Level 03 · Advanced

Growing with intention

Once plants survive, the interesting questions begin: how do I make more of them, shape them, stop pests without chemicals, and build soil that improves every year?

⏱ 20 min read Assumes you have read the basics

1. Propagation: making more from what you have

Every plant has a strategy for copying itself. Learn the four main methods and you rarely need to buy a plant twice.

Stem cuttings

Cut a healthy stem just below a node, remove the lower leaves, and place it in water or damp perlite. Roots appear in 1–4 weeks for pothos, mint, basil and geranium.

Best in springHigh success rate

Division

Dig up a clumping perennial and split the crown with two forks. Each division needs roots and shoots. Ideal for mint, hostas, daylilies and most grasses.

Autumn or early springInstant large plants

Layering & runners

Pin a low stem to the soil and let it root while still attached. Strawberries, spider plants and blackberries do this naturally — pot the runner, then cut it free.

SummerNo cutting needed

Seed saving

Let a healthy plant ripen seed fully, dry it on paper, and store in a labelled envelope in a cool, dark place. Save from open-pollinated varieties, not F1 hybrids.

End of seasonFree plants forever
🌡

Clean cuts, warm roots. Use a sterile blade, keep cuttings at 18–24 °C, and change water every few days. Roots form faster in warmth and bright indirect light — not direct sun.

2. Pruning as design

Advanced pruning is about directing energy. The same cut can create a dense hedge, a fruiting spur, or a sculptural specimen.

Formative pruning (young trees)

Choose 3–5 strong scaffold branches spaced around the trunk, remove competing leaders, and keep the centre open. Do this in the first three years and you will never need drastic corrective pruning.

Fruiting spurs

Apples and pears fruit on short spurs. Cut long laterals back to 3–5 buds in winter to build a spur system; prune gently each year rather than hard every few years.

Espalier & fan training

Tie branches to a wire frame at 45° to slow sap flow and encourage fruiting. Best started on a young tree against a sunny wall. Prune summer for shape, winter for structure.

Pollarding & coppicing

Cut stems back hard on a cycle to produce vigorous young growth — used for willow, hazel and eucalyptus. Only for species that regenerate reliably, and never on conifers.

Bonsai basics

Bonsai is root restriction plus repeated pruning. Use a gritty, free-draining mix, prune roots every 1–3 years, and pinch shoots through the growing season. Species matter more than technique — ficus and Chinese elm are forgiving.

3. Integrated pest management (IPM)

IPM is a ladder, not a spray. Start at the bottom and only climb when necessary.

Prevent

Right plant, right place. Good airflow, correct spacing, clean tools, quarantine new plants for two weeks.

Monitor

Weekly inspections of leaf undersides and new growth. Yellow sticky traps catch early flyers. Keep a note of what appears and when.

Physical control

Remove by hand, blast with water, use barriers, netting, copper tape or beer traps. Often enough for small gardens.

Biological control

Encourage predators: ladybirds, lacewings, hoverflies, parasitic wasps. Plant umbellifers (dill, fennel) and let some herbs flower.

Targeted sprays

Insecticidal soap, neem oil or Bacillus thuringiensis — only where the pest is identified, in the evening, never on flowers in bloom.

4. The soil food web

Healthy soil is a food chain. Bacteria and fungi break down organic matter; protozoa and nematodes eat them and release plant-available nutrients; earthworms and arthropods move everything around.

Bacteria

Dominate in annual beds and compost. Release nitrogen quickly. Encouraged by fresh organic matter and neutral pH.

Mycorrhizal fungi

Extend root reach for water and phosphorus. Damaged by digging and high-phosphorus fertiliser. Support them with mulch and no-dig methods.

Earthworms

Nature's tillers. Their casts are nutrient-rich. Keep soil moist, mulched and undisturbed — and stop using harsh chemicals.

🪱

Feed the soil, not the plant. Compost and mulch build the biology that feeds plants slowly and buffers against drought, disease and pH swings. Synthetic feeds are a supplement, not a foundation.

6. Growing under cover & hydroponics

Greenhouse, polytunnel, cloche

  • Extends the season by 4–8 weeks at each end
  • Needs venting by day even in winter — still, humid air breeds disease
  • Water in the morning; damp foliage overnight is the main risk
  • Shade cloth prevents scorch in strong summer sun

Hydroponics, honestly

  • Soil-free growing in nutrient solution — fast growth, precise control
  • Needs reliable power for pumps and lights; not low-maintenance
  • Best entry point: a simple Kratky jar with lettuce or basil
  • pH and EC meters matter more than expensive equipment

7. Keep records — the real advanced skill

Gardeners who write things down improve dramatically year on year. Record four things and patterns appear fast:

Sow & harvest dates

When did you sow, when did you pick? Shift next year's dates to match.

Weather notes

Frost dates, heatwaves, long wet spells — and what suffered.

Pest timing

Aphids arrive in week 22 every year? Net or plant early next time.

What worked

Variety, position, feed, yield. Repeat the winners; drop the rest.
