Keeping plants alive, then thriving

Water, food, light, air and the occasional haircut. This page turns vague advice like “water when needed” into routines you can actually follow.

⏱ 18 min read Covers indoor and outdoor plants

Watering: the skill that saves plants

Watering is not a schedule, it is a conversation with the soil. The right question is never “how often?” but “is the root zone dry enough yet?” — and the answer changes with pot size, season, light and plant type.

Signs you are underwatering

  • Soil pulls away from the pot edges
  • Leaves droop, then crisp at the tips
  • Lower leaves yellow and drop
  • New growth is small and pale
  • Pot feels very light

Signs you are overwatering

  • Yellowing starts at the lower leaves, soft not crisp
  • Soil stays damp for days; smells sour
  • Brown, mushy stem base
  • Fungus gnats circling the pot
  • Wilting despite wet soil (roots are rotting)
SituationApproach
Small pot (under 15 cm)Check every 2–3 days; dries out fast, especially in sun.
Large pot (30 cm+)Check weekly at 5 cm depth. Water slowly, in two passes, so it soaks rather than runs off.
Summer / active growthMore water, more often. Morning watering is best.
Winter / dormancyCut frequency by half or more. Cool, dark rooms need very little.
Succulents & cactiSoak, then let dry completely. In winter, once a month is plenty.
SeedlingsKeep evenly moist but never soggy. Bottom-water to avoid damping off.
Vacation (1–2 weeks)Move out of direct sun, group pots together, water deeply before leaving. A towel in a water tray works for a week.
💧

Water until it drains. A small splash only wets the surface, encouraging shallow roots. Give enough that water exits the drainage holes, then empty the saucer after 20 minutes.

Feeding: food is not medicine

Fertiliser supplies the minerals a plant cannot make. It cannot fix bad light, soggy roots or pests — and too much is worse than too little. Feed only during active growth: spring and summer for most plants.

N — Nitrogen

Leaf and stem growth. Deficiency: pale older leaves, weak stems. Excess: lush soft growth, few flowers.

P — Phosphorus

Roots, flowers, fruit. Deficiency: purple-tinged leaves, poor flowering.

K — Potassium

Strength, fruiting, disease resistance. Deficiency: brown leaf edges, poor fruit set.

What to use

  • Balanced liquid feed (roughly 10-10-10) — general purpose, every 2–4 weeks.
  • High-potassium — tomatoes, chillies and flowering plants once fruit or buds form.
  • Seaweed extract — gentle tonic, good for transplants and stress recovery.
  • Slow-release granules — convenient for pots; release over 3–6 months.
  • Compost & worm castings — feed the soil life; the most sustainable option.

Golden rules

  • Dilute to half strength and feed more often — safer than full strength.
  • Never feed dry soil: water first, then feed, or you burn roots.
  • Stop feeding in late autumn for most plants.
  • Flush pots with plain water every few months to remove salt build-up.
  • If a plant is sick, fix the cause before feeding it.

Pruning & shaping

Pruning is controlled damage with a purpose: more light, better air, stronger structure, more flowers or fruit. Plants respond to a cut by growing from the nearest buds below it.

Technique 1

Pinching

Nip out the growing tip with thumb and forefinger. Makes plants bushy — ideal for basil, mint, petunias and fuchsias.

Technique 2

Deadheading

Remove spent flowers before they set seed. The plant redirects energy into new blooms instead of fruit.

Technique 3

Thinning & removing dead wood

Cut out crossing, damaged or inward-growing branches at their base. Opens the centre to light and airflow.

Technique 4

Renewal pruning

Cut a third of the oldest stems to the ground each year. Rejuvenates shrubs without losing a whole season of growth.

Technique 5

Hard pruning

Cutting back to a framework. Only for vigorous plants in late winter, and never more than a third in one go.

✂️

Cut cleanly. Use sharp bypass secateurs, make the cut just above an outward-facing bud at a slight angle, and clean blades with alcohol between plants if disease is present.

Repotting without shock

When it is time

  • Roots circle the surface or emerge from drainage holes
  • Water runs straight through and the plant dries out daily
  • Growth has stalled despite good light and feeding
  • Soil is exhausted, compacted or smells sour

Most houseplants need repotting every 1–3 years, ideally in early spring.

How to do it

  1. Water the day before — dry roots tear.
  2. Choose a pot 2–4 cm wider, with drainage.
  3. Loosen the root ball; trim circling roots if pot-bound.
  4. Set at the same depth, backfill with fresh mix, firm gently.
  5. Water, keep out of direct sun for a week, and do not feed for a month.

Pests & diseases

Identify first, then act. Most problems are solved by improving airflow, reducing moisture and removing affected material — not by spraying everything.

The seasonal calendar

Gardening is a year-round rhythm. Adjust months to your local climate — these are typical for temperate and subtropical regions.

Sow & plant

  • Sow tomatoes, chillies, basil and marigold indoors or under cover
  • Plant bare-root shrubs and roses while soil is still cool
  • Direct-sow carrots, radish, lettuce and spinach
  • Divide crowded perennials as new shoots appear

Maintain

  • Feed established plants as growth restarts
  • Repot houseplants before summer
  • Prune spring-flowering shrubs right after bloom
  • Mulch beds to lock in spring moisture; watch for slugs

Water & protect

  • Water deeply in the early morning; mulch to reduce evaporation
  • Shade seedlings and young transplants from midday sun
  • Move houseplants out of hot west-facing windows
  • Check pots daily — they dry far faster than beds

Harvest & upkeep

  • Pick courgettes, beans and cucumbers every other day
  • Deadhead flowers to keep them blooming
  • Feed fruiting crops with high-potassium fertiliser
  • Watch for aphids, whitefly and mildew in still air

Drainage is everything

  • Raise pots on feet; empty saucers after heavy rain
  • Check that beds and planters drain freely
  • Move succulents and cacti under cover
  • Stake tall plants before storms arrive

Disease watch

  • Remove fallen leaves to stop fungal spread
  • Prune for airflow; avoid overhead watering
  • Plant monsoon-friendly greens: taro, colocasia, rice, chillies
  • Check for snails, slugs and root rot weekly

Harvest & store

  • Lift and store root crops before hard frost
  • Save seeds from beans, tomatoes and flowers
  • Plant garlic, tulips and other spring bulbs
  • Sow green manure on empty beds

Prepare for cold

  • Bring tender plants indoors before nights drop below 10 °C
  • Reduce watering and stop feeding as growth slows
  • Mulch perennials and insulate pots
  • Clean and sharpen tools; service the mower

Rest & plan

  • Water sparingly — cool, dim rooms need very little
  • Keep houseplants away from cold draughts and radiators
  • Prune dormant trees and shrubs (not evergreens in frost)
  • Order seeds and plan crop rotations

Protect

  • Fleece or cloche tender plants on frosty nights
  • Knock heavy snow off evergreens
  • Check stored bulbs and tubers for rot
  • Feed the birds — they help with pest control later

Troubleshooting: symptom → cause → fix

Leaves Yellow leaves

Most likely: overwatering (soft, lower leaves) or natural ageing (one or two old leaves).

Check: soil moisture 3 cm down. If wet and yellowing, stop watering and let it dry. If dry and crisp, increase water. Pale yellow between green veins suggests a nutrient deficiency — feed with a balanced liquid feed.

Leaves Brown, crispy tips

Most likely: dry air, inconsistent watering, or salts from tap water.

Fix: raise humidity (pebble tray, grouping), water consistently, and flush the pot with plain water every few months. Trim the brown tips with clean scissors, following the leaf shape.

Growth Leggy, pale, stretched stems

Cause: not enough light. The plant is reaching for it.

Fix: move gradually to a brighter spot over 1–2 weeks, then prune back to a node to encourage bushy regrowth. Rotate the pot weekly.

Growth Lots of leaves, no flowers

Cause: too much nitrogen, too little light, or pruning at the wrong time.

Fix: switch to a high-potassium feed, ensure 6+ hours of sun for sun-lovers, and check whether your plant flowers on old wood (prune after bloom) or new wood (prune in late winter).

Water Wilting even though the soil is wet

Cause: root rot, or roots damaged by cold or fertiliser burn.

Fix: unpot and inspect. Healthy roots are firm and pale; rotten ones are brown and mushy. Cut away the rot, repot in fresh gritty mix with drainage, and water much less.

Water White mould on the soil surface

Cause: usually harmless saprophytic fungus from damp, still air and organic matter.

Fix: scrape it off, let the top layer dry, improve airflow, and water less often. It is a signal, not an emergency.

Pests Sticky residue and tiny insects

Cause: aphids, scale or whitefly excrete honeydew.

Fix: wash the plant, isolate it, and treat undersides with insecticidal soap or neem every 5–7 days for three rounds. Check neighbouring plants.

Environment Leaf drop right after buying

Cause: shock from a change in light, temperature or humidity — very common in new houseplants.

Fix: do nothing dramatic. Keep conditions steady, water normally, and wait two to three weeks. New growth will follow.
