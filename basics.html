Gardening basics

Before you buy a single plant, learn the four things that decide whether it lives: light, water, drainage and timing. This page is the foundation everything else builds on.

⏱ 12 min read No tools required to start

First, the mindset

Gardening is not about having a green thumb. It is about observation. Plants tell you what they need long before they die — a leaf turns pale, a stem leans toward the window, soil pulls away from the pot. The skill is noticing early and changing one thing at a time.

🌿

Change one variable at a time. If you move a plant, change its water and repot it in the same week, you will never know which fix worked. Give each change two weeks.

1. Light: the real limiting factor

Light is food. A plant that gets too little cannot manufacture enough sugar to grow, no matter how well you feed or water it. Before choosing a plant, spend one day watching your spot — check it at 9 am, noon, and 4 pm.

Light levels explained
LevelWhat it looks likeBest for
Full sun6+ hours of direct sun, sharp shadows at middayTomatoes, roses, sunflowers, most herbs
Part sun3–6 hours direct, ideally morning sunLettuce, mint, hydrangea, many flowers
Bright indirectBright enough to read comfortably, but no direct rays on leavesPothos, monstera, ferns, most houseplants
Low lightDim all day; you need a lamp at noonSnake plant, ZZ plant, some ferns — but growth is slow

Three quick tests

  • The shadow test: hold your hand 30 cm above the surface. A sharp shadow means direct sun; a soft, fuzzy shadow means bright indirect.
  • The window rule: in the northern hemisphere, south-facing windows are brightest, north-facing are weakest. East gives gentle morning sun; west gives hot afternoon sun.
  • The phone trick: a free light-meter app is approximate but useful for comparing two spots in the same room.
⚠️

Glass changes everything. Modern double glazing filters much of the UV and reduces intensity. A plant 2 m from a window may be getting only a tenth of the light it would get outdoors.

2. Water: less often, more deeply

Overwatering is the most common way beginners kill plants. Roots need air as much as water; constantly wet soil suffocates them and invites rot. The goal is a cycle: soak thoroughly, then let the top layer dry before watering again.

The finger test

Push a finger 3 cm into the soil. Dry at that depth? Water. Still damp? Wait a day or two. For large pots, use a wooden chopstick — it comes out dark if the soil is wet.

The weight test

Lift the pot right after watering, then again three days later. The difference in weight is a reliable, tool-free moisture gauge once you know your pot.

Rules that prevent most problems

  • Always use a pot with drainage holes. A decorative outer pot is fine only if you empty it 20 minutes after watering.
  • Water the soil, not the leaves. Wet foliage encourages fungal disease, especially indoors.
  • Water in the morning. Leaves dry before night, and the plant has water for the hot part of the day.
  • Room-temperature water. Cold tap water can shock tropical plants.
  • Fewer, deeper waterings build deeper roots than daily sprinkles.

3. Pots, mix and a minimal toolkit

Choosing a pot

  • Size: go one step up only — 2–4 cm wider than the current root ball. Oversized pots stay wet too long.
  • Drainage: at least one hole. More is better.
  • Material: terracotta breathes and dries fast (great for succulents); plastic holds moisture (better for ferns); glazed ceramic is decorative but heavy.
  • Feet or a saucer: never let the base sit in a puddle.

Starter toolkit

  • A trowel (hand shovel)
  • Pruning snips — clean, sharp bypass type
  • A watering can with a fine rose
  • Gloves that actually fit
  • A spray bottle for seedlings and humidity
  • Twine or soft ties for support

You can start with a spoon, a mug and a yoghurt pot. Tools are convenience, not a prerequisite.

4. Pot your first plant, step by step

  1. Prepare the pot

    Cover the drainage hole with a shard or a piece of mesh — not gravel, which actually raises the water table.

  2. Add a base layer of mix

    Fill to about one-third with fresh, damp potting mix. Never use garden soil in a pot; it compacts and drains poorly.

  3. Free the roots

    Slide the plant out, gently loosen a tightly coiled root ball with your fingers. Do not rip healthy roots.

  4. Set the depth

    The top of the root ball should sit 1–2 cm below the rim. Planting too deep rots stems; too shallow exposes roots.

  5. Fill and firm

    Add mix around the sides, pressing lightly to remove air pockets. Leave a 2 cm watering space at the top.

  6. Water to settle

    Water slowly until it runs from the bottom, then top up any sunken mix. Keep it out of harsh sun for three days.

5. Your first four weeks

Week 1

Do almost nothing

Let the plant adjust to its new home. Check soil moisture every second day, but resist feeding or moving it.

Week 2

Establish a watering rhythm

Note how many days it takes the top 3 cm to dry. That interval is your baseline — it will change with weather and season.

Week 3

Look for new growth

A new leaf or shoot means the roots have settled. If nothing happens, check light before changing anything else.

Week 4

Start a light feed

Only for actively growing plants. Half-strength fertiliser once, then monthly through the growing season.

6. Climate, zones and microclimates

Your local climate sets the outer limits of what will grow. Two concepts do most of the work:

  • Hardiness zone — a rough measure of the coldest winter temperature. It tells you which perennials survive outdoors year-round. Tropical plants are grown as annuals or brought inside in cold zones.
  • Microclimate — the conditions of your actual spot. A sheltered courtyard can be a full zone warmer than the open field 200 m away. A balcony can be windier, hotter and drier than the garden below.
🧭

Map your microclimates. Walk your space at different times of day and note: where does frost settle? Where does sun hit first and last? Where does wind funnel? These notes are worth more than any generic zone map.

Glossary of terms you will meet

Annual, biennial, perennial

Annuals complete their life in one year (marigold, basil in cold climates). Biennials grow leaves year one, flower and seed year two (foxglove, carrot). Perennials return every year (rose, mint, most shrubs).

Full sun, part shade, full shade

Full sun = 6+ hours direct. Part shade = 3–6 hours, or dappled light all day. Full shade = less than 3 hours, or bright reflected light only.

Damping off

A fungal disease that collapses seedlings at soil level. Caused by cold, wet, still air. Prevent it with clean containers, good airflow and bottom watering.

Bolt

When a leafy crop suddenly shoots up a flower stalk and turns bitter — usually triggered by heat or day length. Lettuce, coriander and spinach bolt quickly in summer.

Node, internode, auxiliary bud

A node is the point on a stem where leaves and buds emerge. The gap between nodes is the internode. Cuttings must include at least one node to root.

Mulch

A layer of material on top of the soil — bark, straw, compost, leaves. It conserves moisture, suppresses weeds and moderates temperature. Keep it clear of stems to avoid rot.

NPK

The three main nutrients on every fertiliser label: Nitrogen (leaves), Phosphorus (roots, flowers, fruit), Potassium (overall strength, fruiting). A balanced feed is roughly equal parts.

Hardening off

Gradually acclimatising indoor-raised seedlings to outdoor conditions over 7–10 days, starting with a couple of sheltered hours a day. Skipping it causes sunburn and wind damage.
